# Specification Validation and Vacuity Audit

## Purpose

Adversarially checks that a formal specification matches the intended cryptographic object and that accepted theorems are not vacuous, circular, self-referential, or scoped away from the attack surface.

## Use this skill when

Use this skill before trusting a substantial proof and whenever the formal specification was generated by an LLM, extracted from code, or translated from prose/pseudocode with ambiguities.

## Do not invoke automatically

Do not assume that test-vector agreement proves specification fidelity. Vectors exercise a tiny subset and may be shared by two models with the same defect.

## Optional entry contract

**Inputs**
- formal definitions
- source specification or paper
- candidate theorems and examples

**Expected products**
- specification audit
- vacuity findings
- source-to-definition crosswalk
- repair obligations

This skill is an optional specialist route. Completion of an ordinary cryptanalysis task does not require invoking it unless the claim or evidence goal warrants formal methods.

## Operating procedure

1. Build a source-to-formal crosswalk for algorithms, domains, indices, constants, encodings, distributions, errors, and parameter sets. Cite exact source locations and unresolved ambiguities.
2. Check independent derivation. Flag a “specification” that is merely a normalized copy of the implementation when the goal is to prove implementation correctness.
3. Evaluate theorem preconditions for satisfiability and coverage. Construct witnesses satisfying them and measure whether they exclude malformed, weak, boundary, or failure-causing cases central to the claim.
4. Inspect quantifier order and dependencies. Common failures include fixing a key before universal input quantification, replacing adaptive adversaries with static inputs, or proving an average statement while reporting a universal one.
5. Check result strength and relevance: direct equality versus a loose relation, modular versus integer equality, trace equivalence versus final output equality, single execution versus multi-session security, and symbolic versus computational guarantees.
6. Look for circular definitions, reference to the implementation in the mathematical target, imported final theorems, unreachable branches, empty types, contradictory hypotheses, and degenerate default values.
7. Compare multiple implementations, official vectors, independently transcribed pseudocode, and algebraic invariants. Use differential testing to find shared blind spots.
8. Ask an adversarial reviewer to formulate the strongest misleading claim that the theorem could appear to support, then document why that extrapolation is or is not valid.
9. Require corrections to definitions or prose claims before further proof investment; do not patch specification problems with local proof tactics.

## Output contract

- A line- or clause-level source-to-formal crosswalk.
- Vacuity, circularity, coverage, and theorem-strength findings.
- Executable examples and counterexamples for critical domains.
- Repair obligations and a revised assurance statement.

## Non-negotiable guardrails

- A kernel-checked theorem is not evidence that the source transcription is correct.
- Direct equality to an independently authored specification is generally stronger than a postcondition invented from code behavior.
- Do not hide unsupported source choices as “obvious” formalization decisions.
- Flag any theorem whose plain-language summary omits material preconditions or model limitations.
